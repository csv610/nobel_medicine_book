\section*{Main Contribution}

\textit{Severo Ochoa and Marianne Grunberg-Manago discovered an enzyme that could synthesize RNA in vitro, while Arthur Kornberg isolated DNA polymerase and demonstrated enzymatic, template-directed DNA synthesis. Together, these discoveries established nucleic-acid synthesis as a biochemical process.}\cite{nobel-1959,lecture-ochoa-1959,lecture-kornberg-1959}

\section{Background and Historical Context}

The 1959 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was divided equally between Severo Ochoa and Arthur Kornberg ``for their discovery of the mechanisms in the biological synthesis of ribonucleic acid and deoxyribonucleic acid.''\cite{nobel-1959}

By the late 1950s, DNA had been recognized as the hereditary material and its double-helical structure had been described. The genetic code was beginning to be deciphered, but researchers still needed to identify the enzymes that built nucleic-acid chains and to determine how those enzymes used nucleotide substrates and templates.

\subsection{Severo Ochoa}

Severo Ochoa was born on September 24, 1905, in Luarca, Spain. He studied medicine at the University of Madrid and received his medical degree in 1931. After research in Europe, he moved to the United States in 1940, where he worked at Washington University in St. Louis and later at New York University. His research included energy metabolism and the enzymology of nucleic acids.

\subsection{Arthur Kornberg}

Arthur Kornberg was born on March 3, 1918, in Brooklyn, New York. He received a bachelor's degree from the College of the City of New York in 1937 and an M.D. from the University of Rochester in 1941. After clinical training, he joined the National Institutes of Health, where he pursued the search for enzymes that synthesize DNA. He later moved to Washington University and then Stanford University.

\subsection{The Nucleic-Acid Synthesis Problem}

Nucleotides are chemically complex, and a cell must assemble them into long chains with defined linkages and, for genetic replication and transcription, controlled sequence information. The challenge was to isolate the responsible enzymes and distinguish genuine polymerization from nonspecific chemical incorporation.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

Ochoa and Kornberg provided complementary experimental systems. Ochoa's work revealed a powerful in-vitro reaction for making polyribonucleotides from nucleotide diphosphates. Kornberg isolated a template-dependent DNA polymerase that copied DNA using deoxynucleoside triphosphates.

\subsection{Ochoa and Grunberg-Manago: Polynucleotide Phosphorylase}

In 1955, Marianne Grunberg-Manago and Ochoa reported an enzyme from \textit{Azotobacter vinelandii} that could synthesize polyribonucleotides from ribonucleoside diphosphates. The enzyme was named polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase). Grunberg-Manago made the initial discovery and was a principal contributor to the work; the Nobel recognition, however, went to Ochoa.

The reaction is reversible and can be represented schematically as
\[
\text{RNA}_{n} + \text{ribonucleoside diphosphate} \rightleftharpoons \text{RNA}_{n+1} + \text{P}_{\mathrm{i}}.
\]
Under conditions rich in nucleotide diphosphates and favorable for the right-hand direction, PNPase can synthesize RNA chains and release inorganic phosphate. Unlike a true template-directed RNA polymerase, it does not normally copy a DNA template to produce a defined messenger-RNA sequence.

\subsection{Importance and Limitation of PNPase}

PNPase provided one of the first experimentally useful systems for enzymatic RNA synthesis. Ochoa's laboratory and other groups used the enzyme to prepare synthetic polyribonucleotides, including polymers with defined base composition. These materials became important in experiments that helped decipher the genetic code.

Later work showed that PNPase is not the principal DNA-dependent RNA polymerase that transcribes genes in living cells. In many bacteria, PNPase functions mainly as a 3-prime-to-5-prime phosphorolytic exoribonuclease involved in RNA degradation and processing, although it can also have biosynthetic roles under particular conditions. The discovery was therefore a major biochemical tool and mechanistic advance, not a complete explanation of physiological RNA transcription.

\subsection{Kornberg and DNA Polymerase I}

Kornberg and his colleagues developed an assay that measured incorporation of radioactive deoxynucleotides into DNA. They used it to purify an enzyme from \textit{Escherichia coli}, reported in 1956 and later called DNA polymerase I.

DNA polymerase I uses deoxynucleoside triphosphates to extend a pre-existing primer paired with a complementary DNA template. It adds nucleotides to the 3-prime end of the growing strand, releases pyrophosphate, and follows the template's base sequence. The enzyme therefore demonstrated that DNA synthesis could be reproduced and studied outside the living cell.

\subsection{Activities of DNA Polymerase I}

Kornberg's work and subsequent studies established that DNA polymerase I has more than one enzymatic activity. Its 5-prime-to-3-prime polymerase activity extends DNA, its 3-prime-to-5-prime exonuclease activity can proofread some newly added bases, and its 5-prime-to-3-prime exonuclease activity can remove nucleotides, including RNA-primer segments in appropriate repair or replication contexts.

DNA polymerase I is not the main chromosomal replicase of \textit{E. coli}; DNA polymerase III performs that primary function. This later distinction did not diminish Kornberg's discovery, which supplied the first purified system for studying DNA polymerization and established key chemical principles of replication.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The discoveries of Ochoa and Kornberg made nucleic-acid synthesis experimentally accessible and helped launch molecular medicine, diagnostic genetics, biotechnology, and antiviral pharmacology.

\subsection{DNA Replication and Genetic Disease}

The study of DNA polymerases revealed how mutations, replication errors, repair defects, and checkpoint failures can affect genome stability. These principles are relevant to inherited disorders, cancer, aging, and the development of drugs that interfere with DNA synthesis.

\subsection{Polymerase Chain Reaction}

PCR was developed by Kary Mullis and colleagues in the 1980s. It uses repeated cycles of denaturation, primer annealing, and extension by a thermostable DNA polymerase to amplify selected DNA sequences. PCR is not a direct invention of Kornberg, but it depends on the enzymatic principles and polymerase assays that his work helped establish.

PCR is used in genetic testing, infectious-disease diagnosis, forensic analysis, prenatal testing, cancer genotyping, and research. Related amplification and sequencing methods have expanded the reach of nucleic-acid enzymology into routine medicine.

\subsection{RNA Metabolism and Gene Expression}

Ochoa's PNPase work provided synthetic RNA substrates and helped show that enzymes can assemble RNA chains. Later discovery and characterization of DNA-dependent RNA polymerases, ribonucleases, ribosomes, and RNA-processing systems supplied the physiological mechanisms of transcription and RNA turnover.

\subsection{Nucleic-Acid Therapies}

Modern antisense oligonucleotides, small-interfering RNAs, messenger-RNA medicines, and gene therapies all depend on the chemistry, synthesis, delivery, and degradation of nucleic acids. These technologies are later developments with many contributors, but they rest on the ability to study nucleic-acid chains as defined biochemical molecules.

\subsection{Antiviral and Anticancer Drugs}

Many antiviral drugs inhibit viral polymerases or related enzymes. Acyclovir targets herpesvirus DNA polymerase after activation in infected cells, while nucleotide analogues such as tenofovir interfere with viral reverse transcriptases. These drugs were developed through later medicinal chemistry and virology, but their mechanisms are understood through the broader enzymology of nucleic-acid synthesis.

\section{Legacy}

Ochoa's and Kornberg's discoveries replaced the idea that nucleic-acid synthesis was an inaccessible property of living cells with experimentally testable enzyme reactions. Their assays allowed researchers to vary substrates, templates, cofactors, and inhibitors and to analyze the chemistry of chain growth.

PNPase became an important tool in deciphering the genetic code even though its main physiological roles were later recognized as different from those first proposed. DNA polymerase I became a model for replication, repair, proofreading, and enzyme purification. Together, these systems helped establish the biochemical foundations of molecular biology.

\subsection{Recognition and Honors}

Ochoa and Kornberg shared the 1959 Nobel Prize equally. Both received major scientific honors, including the U.S. National Medal of Science and election to the National Academy of Sciences. Their laboratories also trained scientists who contributed to later advances in nucleic-acid biology and molecular medicine.

\section{Modern Developments}

DNA polymerases and RNA polymerases are now used in PCR, sequencing, cloning, gene-expression analysis, and production of selected biotechnology products. High-fidelity polymerases, reverse transcriptases, strand-displacing enzymes, and engineered polymerases have expanded the range of nucleic-acid assays.

Modern replication research includes replisome architecture, lesion bypass, proofreading, mismatch repair, single-molecule imaging, and polymerase inhibitors. RNA biology now encompasses transcription, processing, decay, noncoding RNAs, RNA modifications, and therapeutic mRNA. Ochoa and Kornberg's work remains foundational, but current understanding depends on many subsequent discoveries beyond the enzymes they first isolated.
