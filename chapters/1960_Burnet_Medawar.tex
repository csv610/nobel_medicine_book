\section{Background and Historical Context}

The 1960 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Frank Macfarlane Burnet and Peter Brian Medawar ``for discovery of acquired immunological tolerance.''\cite{nobel-1960}

The prize recognized complementary theoretical and experimental work. Burnet proposed that immune cells carry individually specific receptors and that selection of those cells explains the specificity of immune responses. Medawar's laboratory then showed that exposure to donor cells during a critical early period could make an animal accept later grafts from that donor while rejecting grafts from unrelated strains.

The work changed immunology's understanding of self-recognition and transplantation. It did not by itself produce a generally applicable method for making adult human recipients permanently tolerant of transplanted organs, nor did it establish a single complete explanation for every autoimmune disease.

\subsection{The Immunology Revolution}

By the middle of the twentieth century, antibodies, lymphoid tissues, and transplantation rejection were well documented, but the cellular basis of specificity and self--non-self discrimination remained uncertain. The tolerance experiments built on earlier observations, including Ray Owen's work on cattle twins, and provided a controlled experimental demonstration in mice.

\subsection{Frank Macfarlane Burnet}

Frank Macfarlane Burnet was born on September 3, 1899, in Traralgon, Australia. He studied medicine at the University of Melbourne and joined the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research, where he made important contributions to virology and immunology. His clonal-selection ideas became a major framework for explaining how an enormous repertoire of lymphocytes can generate specific immune responses.

\subsection{Peter Brian Medawar}

Peter Brian Medawar was born on February 28, 1915, in Petr\'opolis, Brazil. He studied at the University of Oxford and investigated tissue growth and wound healing before turning to transplantation immunity during and after the Second World War. He later held positions at the University of Birmingham and University College London.

Medawar's transplantation studies were carried out with research fellows and students, especially Rupert E. Billingham and Leslie Brent. Their collaborative work was essential to the experimental demonstration of actively acquired tolerance.

\subsection{The Problem of Transplant Rejection}

A graft from a genetically different individual is usually recognized as foreign and attacked by the recipient's immune system. Before effective immunosuppression and tissue matching, rejection was a major barrier to transplantation. A central question was whether the immune system could be instructed during development to regard selected foreign tissue as acceptable.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

\subsection{Burnet's Clonal-Selection Theory}

Burnet's clonal-selection theory, developed in the 1950s, proposed that immune cells bear receptors with different specificities. An antigen selects cells with complementary receptors, which then proliferate and generate effector and memory populations. In later versions of the theory, developing lymphocytes that strongly recognize self can be deleted or functionally silenced.

The theory explained specificity and immunological memory, and it offered a mechanism by which tolerance could arise. Its early formulations were theoretical and did not identify all of the cellular and molecular mechanisms now known to contribute to tolerance, including regulatory cells, anergy, peripheral deletion, and tissue-specific regulation.

\subsection{Medawar, Billingham, and Brent: Experimental Tolerance}

Medawar's group used skin grafts as a measurable test of transplantation immunity. In the experiments reported in 1953, Billingham, Brent, and Medawar introduced cells from one inbred mouse strain into fetuses or newborn mice of another strain. When the recipients matured, they accepted skin grafts from the original donor strain but rejected grafts from a third strain.

This was specific acquired tolerance: the animals had not become generally immunodeficient. The timing of cell exposure was crucial, and the strength and duration of tolerance varied with the strain combination, cell dose, route, and experimental conditions. The original experiments therefore demonstrated a powerful principle, not a universal guarantee of lifelong tolerance in every setting.

\subsection{Mechanisms of Tolerance}

Burnet and Medawar interpreted the findings in terms of immune recognition during development. Clonal deletion became one important explanation for central tolerance, but later research showed that tolerance is maintained by several overlapping mechanisms. These include deletion, anergy, ignorance, regulatory T cells, inhibitory receptors, tissue barriers, and controlled presentation of antigen in peripheral tissues.

\subsection{The Combined Contribution}

Burnet supplied a theory that predicted how the immune system could acquire specificity and tolerance. Medawar, Billingham, and Brent supplied the decisive transplantation experiments. The Nobel Prize was awarded to Burnet and Medawar, while the historical record should also preserve the indispensable experimental contributions of Billingham and Brent.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The discovery of acquired tolerance influenced transplantation immunology, autoimmune-disease research, vaccine science, and the development of therapies that manipulate lymphocyte activation.

\subsection{Organ Transplantation}

The tolerance experiments showed that rejection was an immune process that could, in principle, be modified by timing and exposure to donor antigens. Clinical transplantation instead advanced through a combination of donor selection, HLA and blood-group compatibility, surgical technique, antimicrobial care, and immunosuppressive drugs.

Calcineurin inhibitors, corticosteroids, antimetabolites, mTOR inhibitors, antibody therapies, and costimulation blockers can prevent rejection, but most recipients still require ongoing immunosuppression and remain at risk of infection, malignancy, drug toxicity, and chronic graft injury. Belatacept, for example, blocks CD80/CD86--CD28 costimulation in selected kidney-transplant patients; it is an immunosuppressive therapy, not proof of durable antigen-specific tolerance.

\subsection{Autoimmune Disease}

The concept of self-tolerance provides a framework for understanding how immune responses against self can arise when central deletion, peripheral regulation, tissue barriers, or antigen presentation are disrupted. Autoimmune diseases are heterogeneous, and their causes include genetic susceptibility, infection, environmental triggers, and defects in multiple tolerance mechanisms.

Modern treatments include broad immunosuppression, targeted biologics, antigen-specific approaches under investigation, and cell therapies. Most current treatments control disease rather than permanently restoring normal tolerance.

\subsection{Immunodeficiency and Vaccination}

Clonal selection and tolerance theory helped organize research on lymphocyte development, immunodeficiency, and memory. Vaccines exploit antigen-specific expansion and memory rather than tolerance: they are designed to produce protective immunity while avoiding harmful responses. This distinction is important because immune activation and immune tolerance are related but opposing therapeutic goals.

\subsection{Tolerance-Based Therapies}

Researchers are investigating regulatory T-cell therapy, tolerogenic antigen delivery, mixed hematopoietic chimerism, costimulation blockade, and tissue-engineering strategies. Some have entered clinical trials or selected clinical applications, but durable, antigen-specific tolerance remains difficult to achieve safely in most adult transplant and autoimmune settings.

\section{Legacy}

Burnet and Medawar helped establish immune tolerance as a central principle of biology. Burnet's theory supplied a cellular explanation for specificity, while Medawar's collaboration with Billingham and Brent demonstrated that tolerance could be acquired experimentally and maintained with donor specificity.

The work also showed how fundamental immunology and clinical transplantation interact. The mouse experiments could not simply be transferred to adult human organ recipients, but they changed the questions that transplantation researchers asked and motivated decades of work on compatibility, immunosuppression, chimerism, and immune regulation.

\subsection{Foundation for Transplantation Immunology}

The tolerance concept helped motivate later studies of histocompatibility antigens, including the HLA system, and of lymphocyte activation and regulation. Modern transplantation is therefore the product of many advances rather than a direct application of one experimental protocol.

\subsection{Immunology as a Science}

Clonal selection remains a useful organizing concept, but modern immunology adds antigen presentation, T-cell receptor signaling, B-cell maturation, regulatory lymphocytes, innate immunity, cytokine networks, and tissue-specific immune regulation. The field has moved from a simple self--non-self model toward a more context-dependent understanding of immune responses.

\subsection{Recognition and Honors}

Burnet and Medawar shared the 1960 Nobel Prize and received many additional honors. Burnet was appointed to the Order of Merit and elected to the Royal Society. Medawar was also appointed to the Order of Merit and elected to the Royal Society. Their scientific legacy includes both the theory and the collaborative experimental practice that made acquired tolerance a testable biological phenomenon.

\section{Modern Developments}

Transplant research now combines HLA matching, molecular donor-specific-antibody testing, minimally invasive monitoring, targeted immunosuppression, and attempts to induce mixed chimerism or regulatory tolerance. Regulatory T-cell products and antigen-specific tolerogenic therapies remain active areas of investigation.

Immune-checkpoint inhibitors illustrate the opposite therapeutic direction: blocking inhibitory pathways can restore antitumor immunity but may also break peripheral tolerance and cause immune-related adverse events. This contrast shows how the same principles of immune regulation can be used either to suppress unwanted responses or to intensify protective ones.
