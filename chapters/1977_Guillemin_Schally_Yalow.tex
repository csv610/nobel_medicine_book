\section[Roger Guillemin and Andrew V.]{Roger Guillemin and Andrew V. Schally: Discoveries Concerning the Peptide Hormone Production of the Brain}

\subsection{Background and Historical Context}

The 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was divided: one half was awarded jointly to Roger Guillemin and Andrew V. Schally ``for their discoveries concerning the peptide hormone production of the brain,'' and the other half was awarded to Rosalyn Yalow ``for her development of the radioimmunoassay of peptide hormones.'' Guillemin and Schally established important links between hypothalamic peptides and pituitary regulation, while Yalow and Solomon Berson created a sensitive method for measuring hormones in blood.

For much of the twentieth century, the relationship between the brain and the endocrine system was poorly understood. It was known that the pituitary gland, a small organ at the base of the brain, produced hormones that regulated growth, reproduction, metabolism, and other vital functions. It was also known that the hypothalamus, a region of the brain just above the pituitary, somehow controlled the pituitary's activity. However, the mechanism by which the hypothalamus communicated with the pituitary---and the nature of the hypothalamic ``releasing hormones'' that regulated pituitary function---remained unknown.

Roger Guillemin was born in Dijon, France, in 1924, and trained in medicine at the University of Lyon. He emigrated to the United States in 1949 and joined the laboratory of Hans Selye in Montreal before moving to the Baylor College of Medicine in Houston, Texas. Andrew V. Schally was born in Wilno, Poland (now Vilnius, Lithuania), in 1926, and trained in medicine at the University of London. He also emigrated to the United States, joining the Baylor College of Medicine in 1957, where he and Guillemin were colleagues for a period before Schally moved to the Veterans Administration Hospital in New Orleans.

The effort to identify hypothalamic releasing hormones was one of the most demanding and competitive episodes in endocrinology. Guillemin and Schally were principal rivals in this work, and their independent approaches helped establish the structures and functions of several hypothalamic peptides.

\subsection{The Discovery/Contribution}

The identification of hypothalamic releasing hormones was a monumental biochemical challenge. These peptides occur in extremely small amounts, so isolating and characterizing them required processing very large numbers of animal hypothalami. Guillemin and Schally each organized massive extraction projects and developed increasingly sensitive purification and sequencing methods.

Schally and Guillemin's groups independently isolated and characterized thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) around 1969. TRH is a tripeptide (pyroGlu-His-ProNH$_2$) that stimulates release of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and can also influence prolactin secretion. Determining its structure required the purification of very large numbers of animal hypothalami.

Shortly afterward, Guillemin and Schally's groups independently isolated and characterized luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH), now usually called gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). GnRH is a decapeptide released in pulses that regulates pituitary secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), thereby influencing reproductive function. Its structure was established through biochemical analysis and chemical synthesis.

Guillemin's group, working at the Salk Institute in La Jolla, also studied corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), growth-hormone-releasing factors, and somatostatin. The structures and physiological roles of these peptides were established through work by Guillemin, Schally, and many other investigators over subsequent years; the 1977 award should not be read as crediting one laureate alone for every hypothalamic hormone.

The identification of these hypothalamic peptides established a new principle in endocrinology: that the brain produces hormones that regulate the pituitary gland, and that these hormones are small peptides that act as chemical messengers between the nervous system and the endocrine system. This discovery---the concept of neuroendocrine regulation---fundamentally changed our understanding of how the body maintains hormonal homeostasis. It also opened the door to the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic tools for disorders of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis.

\section[Rosalyn Yalow: Development of the]{Rosalyn Yalow: Development of the Radioimmunoassay of Peptide Hormones}

\subsection{Background and Historical Context}

Rosalyn Sussman Yalow was born in New York City in 1921 and trained in physics at the University of Illinois, earning her Ph.D. in 1945. She joined the Veterans Administration Hospital in the Bronx in 1950, where she worked with Solomon Berson on the study of insulin metabolism in diabetic patients. Yalow's background in physics gave her a unique perspective on biological measurement, and she brought the quantitative rigor of physics to the study of hormone biology.

\subsection{The Discovery/Contribution}

The radioimmunoassay (RIA) technique that Yalow developed with Berson was a major methodological innovation. Their studies found that insulin administered to some patients was bound by antibodies produced after exposure to exogenous insulin. Berson and Yalow realized that this antibody binding could be exploited to measure insulin in blood samples with high sensitivity.

The principle of the radioimmunoassay is elegant in its simplicity. A fixed amount of radioactively labeled hormone (the ``tracer'') is mixed with a fixed amount of antibody specific for that hormone, along with varying amounts of unlabeled hormone (the ``standard'' or the unknown sample). The labeled and unlabeled hormones compete for binding to the antibody. The more unlabeled hormone present, the less labeled hormone is bound. By measuring the fraction of labeled hormone bound to the antibody, the concentration of unlabeled hormone in the sample can be determined with great precision.

Yalow and Berson published their first description of radioimmunoassay for insulin in 1959, and the technique was quickly recognized as a significant advance. Before RIA, direct measurement of insulin at physiological concentrations was difficult. RIA enabled highly sensitive quantitative measurements, with the achievable range depending on the hormone, reagents, and assay design.

Yalow's group went on to develop radioimmunoassays for many hormones and other biologically active substances, including growth hormone, parathyroid hormone, ACTH, angiotensin, and cyclic AMP. The broader RIA methodology was later adapted to drugs, vitamins, and some viral antigens, including hepatitis B surface antigen; clinical blood screening has also relied heavily on non-radioactive immunoassays.

Yalow was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1977 for her development of radioimmunoassays of peptide hormones. She was the second woman to receive the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, after Gerty Cori; her half of the 1977 prize was awarded for the RIA work, while the other half was shared by Guillemin and Schally.

Berson, Yalow's collaborator and close scientific partner, had died of cancer in 1972, five years before the prize was awarded. Nobel Prizes are not given posthumously, so Berson could not share in the honor, but Yalow always emphasized that the development of the radioimmunoassay was a collaborative achievement.

\subsection{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The impact of radioimmunoassay on medicine was substantial. It transformed clinical endocrinology by making it possible to measure many hormones in blood with high sensitivity. Before RIA, clinicians often relied on biological effects, stimulation tests, or less direct measurements. Hormone assays contributed to the evaluation of disorders such as diabetes, thyroid disease, growth abnormalities, and pituitary disease, alongside clinical assessment and other laboratory tests.

The technique also had a significant impact on basic research. By enabling sensitive measurement of hormones in blood and tissues, RIA helped researchers study hormone secretion, pharmacokinetics, and endocrine disease. Related immunoassays and immunochemical methods also contributed to research on neuropeptides and brain chemistry.

Beyond endocrinology, RIA was adapted to measure therapeutic drugs, vitamins, viral antigens, and other analytes. Some assays supported therapeutic-drug monitoring and hepatitis B surface-antigen testing. HIV blood screening, however, has primarily used enzyme-linked and other non-radioactive immunoassays rather than RIA. Related immunoassay technologies have also been used in research, forensic science, environmental monitoring, and food testing.

The development of RIA helped motivate later non-radioactive immunoassay techniques, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and chemiluminescent immunoassays, which have largely replaced RIA in many clinical laboratories. Competitive binding remains one important immunoassay format, but many modern assays use noncompetitive or sandwich designs. Yalow's contribution therefore lies in establishing a powerful quantitative immunoassay principle and demonstrating its biomedical value, not in directly creating every later assay.

\section{Legacy}

The work of Guillemin, Schally, and Yalow helped transform endocrinology into a quantitative molecular science. Guillemin and Schally's identification of hypothalamic releasing hormones clarified chemical communication between the brain and pituitary and provided a foundation for modern neuroendocrinology. Synthetic GnRH analogs are now used in conditions including prostate cancer, endometriosis, infertility, and precocious puberty; somatostatin analogs are used for acromegaly and some neuroendocrine tumors. These therapies were developed through later pharmacological and clinical research.

Yalow's radioimmunoassay became a widely used technique in research, endocrinology, pharmacology, and diagnostic medicine. Yalow continued her work at the Bronx VA Medical Center for decades and became an advocate for women in science and a critic of pseudoscience.

Together, the work of the three 1977 laureates demonstrated the power of biochemical and biophysical approaches to solve fundamental problems in medicine. Their discoveries about the peptide hormones of the brain and the techniques for measuring them have saved countless lives and continue to shape our understanding of the endocrine system and its role in health and disease.


\section{Modern Developments}

Guillemin, Schally, and Yalow's work helped establish modern neuroendocrinology and hormone measurement. Understanding of GnRH informs treatments such as leuprolide for prostate cancer, endometriosis, and precocious puberty. Somatostatin analogs such as octreotide are used for acromegaly and some neuroendocrine tumors. Later peptide-drug development, including GLP-1 receptor agonists for diabetes and obesity, reflects the broader maturation of peptide endocrinology rather than a direct extension of one laureate's discovery.
