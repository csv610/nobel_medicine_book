\section{Background and Historical Context}

Thomas Hunt Morgan was born on September 25, 1866, in Lexington, Kentucky. He studied at the University of Kentucky and Johns Hopkins University, where he received his doctorate in 1890 under William Keith Brooks. His early work concerned embryology, development, and regeneration, including research at the Naples Zoological Station.

In 1904 Morgan joined Columbia University. He initially doubted that Mendelian factors were located on chromosomes, but his experiments with the fruit fly \emph{Drosophila melanogaster} changed his view. In 1928 he moved to the California Institute of Technology, where he became the first chairman of its Biology Division. His Columbia laboratory included Alfred Sturtevant, Calvin Bridges, and Hermann J. Muller, whose work was essential to the development of classical genetics.

Mendel's laws had been rediscovered around 1900, but the physical basis of hereditary factors remained uncertain. Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri had proposed a chromosome theory of inheritance in 1902--1903. Morgan's group supplied powerful experimental and cytological evidence connecting Mendelian inheritance, chromosome behavior, sex linkage, non-disjunction, and linkage groups.

\section{The White-Eye Mutation and Sex Linkage}

In 1910 Morgan found a white-eyed male among normally red-eyed \emph{Drosophila}. Crosses involving this mutant produced a sex-linked inheritance pattern: the white-eye trait was associated with the X chromosome and appeared much more often in males because males have one X chromosome. The result provided strong evidence that a hereditary factor could be associated with a particular chromosome.

The white-eye experiments were not, by themselves, a complete proof of the chromosome theory. Their force came from the way they fit other results from Morgan's group, including linkage, recombination, and Calvin Bridges's later cytological analysis of exceptional offspring caused by chromosome non-disjunction. The evidence made the chromosome theory a powerful explanation of Mendelian inheritance rather than a mere analogy between chromosomes and hereditary factors.

\section{Linkage, Recombination, and Genetic Maps}

Morgan and his students found that some traits were inherited together because their genes were in the same linkage group. Linkage was incomplete: offspring could receive new combinations when homologous chromosomes recombined during meiosis. Morgan's work helped establish the genetic significance of crossing-over, while the physical details of chiasmata and chromosome exchange were clarified by later cytological research.

In 1913 Alfred Sturtevant, then a student in Morgan's laboratory, used recombination frequencies to construct the first genetic map. The frequency of recombination between markers provided a relative measure of their distance, allowing genes to be arranged in a linear order. Genetic distance is not identical to physical distance in base pairs, and recombination rates vary along chromosomes, but the mapping principle became foundational to genetics.

Morgan, Sturtevant, Bridges, and Muller synthesized this work in \emph{The Mechanism of Mendelian Heredity} (1915). Their collaboration helped establish \emph{Drosophila} as a model organism and showed how breeding experiments, microscopy, and quantitative analysis could be combined.

\section{The Nobel Prize}

Morgan received the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine ``for his discoveries concerning the role played by the chromosome in heredity.'' The prize recognized Morgan's body of work, including the experimental genetics developed with his laboratory. It did not mean that Morgan alone discovered every feature of chromosome biology or that the molecular structure of genes was already known. DNA as the hereditary material and the molecular basis of genes were established through later research.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The chromosome theory became a foundation of medical genetics. Linkage analysis and cytogenetics later helped researchers locate disease-associated genes, identify chromosomal abnormalities, and study inherited patterns. Modern gene mapping, genome sequencing, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and molecular diagnostics extend these principles with DNA-based methods that were unavailable to Morgan.

Cancer genetics also uses the idea that changes in genes and chromosomes can alter cell behavior. Mutations, copy-number changes, chromosomal rearrangements, and epigenetic alterations contribute to different cancers. This is an intellectual descendant of chromosomal genetics, not a simple direct application of a single \emph{Drosophila} experiment.

Gene therapy and CRISPR-based editing likewise depend on later discoveries about DNA, gene regulation, vectors, nucleases, and cell biology. They can be described as part of the broader history of genetics, but not as technologies Morgan himself anticipated in their modern form.

\section{Legacy}

Morgan transformed genetics by making heredity experimentally testable at the level of chromosomes and traits. His group showed that genes could be arranged in linkage groups and mapped by recombination, while Bridges's cytological work connected genetic predictions to visible chromosome behavior. The contributions of these collaborators should not be absorbed into Morgan's name alone.

Morgan's laboratory also demonstrated the value of model organisms. \emph{Drosophila} remains useful because it has a short generation time, many offspring, a tractable genome, and powerful genetic tools. The success of the fly room did not mean that every result in flies transfers directly to humans; model organisms reveal mechanisms that must be tested in appropriate human and clinical contexts.

Morgan served as president of the National Academy of Sciences from 1927 to 1931 and continued research at Caltech. He died in Pasadena, California, on December 4, 1945. His enduring contribution is the experimental framework linking inheritance, chromosomes, recombination, and genetic maps.

\section{Modern Developments}

Modern genetics combines linkage maps with physical maps, whole-genome sequencing, comparative genomics, single-cell methods, and chromosome imaging. The Human Genome Project and later reference assemblies greatly extended the scale of mapping, while genome-wide association studies and sequencing identify variants associated with disease.

These technologies also show the limits of the early ``beads on a string'' picture. Genes interact with regulatory elements, chromatin, RNA, environmental signals, and one another; chromosomes are dynamic structures rather than simple linear lists. Morgan's work remains foundational because it established the relationship between hereditary variation and chromosome behavior, not because it supplied a complete modern theory of gene function.
