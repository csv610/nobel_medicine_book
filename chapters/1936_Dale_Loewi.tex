\section{Background and Historical Context}

Henry Hallett Dale was born on June 9, 1875, in London. He studied at Cambridge and St Bartholomew's Hospital, researched pharmacology and physiology, and directed the Wellcome Physiological Research Laboratories before becoming director of the National Institute for Medical Research. His work included histamine, ergot alkaloids, acetylcholine, and the pharmacology of autonomic nerves.

Otto Loewi was born on June 3, 1873, in Frankfurt. He studied medicine at Strasbourg and worked in pharmacology before becoming professor at Graz. He later emigrated after the annexation of Austria and continued his research in the United States. The two scientists' work converged on a major question: whether the effects of nerve impulses were mediated only by electrical phenomena or also by chemical substances.

The eventual answer was not either/or. Nerve fibers conduct electrical impulses, and many synapses transmit through chemical release. Electrical synapses, mediated by gap junctions, also exist. Dale and Loewi's Nobel-winning work established the importance of chemical transmission in a period when its role was disputed.

\section{Loewi's Frog-Heart Experiment}

In 1921 Loewi stimulated the vagus nerve of an isolated frog heart while the heart was perfused with a nutrient solution. The heart slowed. When he transferred the solution to a second frog heart, the second heart also slowed, even though its vagus nerve had not been stimulated. Loewi called the active substance ``Vagusstoff'' or vagus substance.

The result showed that vagal stimulation released a transferable chemical messenger into the perfusate. It did not demonstrate that all nerve impulses are chemical, nor did it identify the transmitter immediately. Loewi and collaborators showed that the effect was blocked by atropine, rapidly destroyed by an esterase, and potentiated by eserine, an esterase inhibitor. These observations made acetylcholine the leading candidate.

The famous dream story is part of Loewi's later account, but the scientific evidence rested on repeated experiments and subsequent pharmacological analysis. Historical anecdotes should not replace the experimental record.

\section{Dale and Acetylcholine}

Dale had studied acetylcholine and its physiological actions before Loewi's experiment. Dale, H. W. Dudley, and other colleagues later identified acetylcholine as a naturally occurring substance and compared its actions with those of Vagusstoff. The matching effects, rapid enzymatic breakdown, and pharmacological behavior supported the identification.

Dale also developed the functional terms ``cholinergic'' and ``adrenergic'' for nerve fibers whose effects were mediated by acetylcholine-like or adrenaline-like chemical transmitters. His work helped distinguish muscarinic and nicotinic pharmacological actions of acetylcholine. These terms and distinctions described patterns of drug response; the molecular receptor subtypes and their structures were established by later pharmacology and molecular biology.

The generalizations were useful but not absolute. Most parasympathetic postganglionic transmission is cholinergic, while many sympathetic postganglionic fibers are noradrenergic, but there are important exceptions. Acetylcholine is not the transmitter at every synapse, and chemical transmission does not exclude electrical transmission.

\section{The Nobel Prize}

Dale and Loewi shared the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine ``for their discoveries relating to chemical transmission of nerve impulses.'' Loewi supplied the decisive transfer experiment in the frog heart; Dale's pharmacological and biochemical work helped establish the identity, actions, and broader significance of acetylcholine and related transmitters. Their contributions were complementary and depended on work by other investigators as well.

\section{Impact on Medicine}

The chemical-transmission principle became a foundation of neuropharmacology. Drugs can alter neurotransmitter synthesis, release, breakdown, receptor activation, or reuptake. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are used in several settings, including myasthenia gravis and symptomatic treatment of some forms of dementia, but their clinical development required later work and they do not reverse the underlying diseases.

Muscarinic and nicotinic antagonists and agonists are used in anesthesia, ophthalmology, respiratory medicine, autonomic disorders, and neuromuscular practice. Adrenergic pharmacology later contributed to beta-blockers, alpha-acting drugs, and bronchodilators. These are descendants of the pharmacological framework that Dale helped establish, not direct products of Loewi's frog-heart experiment.

The recognition of chemical signaling also supported the later discovery of many neurotransmitters, including noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, GABA, glutamate, and neuropeptides. Each system has distinct synthesis, release, receptors, transporters, and clinical effects. The history should therefore avoid presenting acetylcholine as a universal explanation of neural communication.

\section{Legacy}

Loewi's experiment is a model of a strong physiological test: stimulation of one organ produced a transferable effect in another preparation. Dale's work shows how pharmacology can identify and classify endogenous signaling molecules by their actions and susceptibility to antagonists and enzymes.

Their work helped establish chemical neurotransmission without reducing the nervous system to a purely chemical circuit. Modern neuroscience recognizes chemical, electrical, and mixed synapses, as well as non-synaptic and volume transmission. This broader view preserves the importance of Dale and Loewi while avoiding the false claim that their work excluded electrical communication.

Dale was knighted in 1932 and served as president of the Royal Society. He died in London on July 23, 1968. Loewi died in New York on December 25, 1961. Their legacy remains visible in autonomic pharmacology, neuromuscular medicine, and the study of how electrical activity is converted into chemical signals and back again.

\section{Modern Developments}

Modern neuropharmacology studies transmitter release, receptor subtypes, synaptic plasticity, transporters, and interactions between chemical and electrical synapses. Cholinesterase inhibitors, neuromuscular blockers, adrenergic drugs, and many psychiatric medicines act on systems that developed from this broader field, but their indications and limitations are determined by later clinical trials.

Optogenetics and related circuit tools can control defined neurons, yet they do not simply reproduce Dale and Loewi's experiments. Current research also recognizes mixed synapses and electrical coupling in the mammalian nervous system. The enduring lesson is that nerve signaling is electrochemical: electrical impulses trigger chemical events at many synapses, and those chemical events alter electrical activity in the next cell.
