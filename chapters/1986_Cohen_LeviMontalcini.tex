\section{Official Citation}

for their discoveries of growth factors

The 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Stanley Cohen and Rita Levi-Montalcini for their discoveries of growth factors. Their work on NGF and EGF helped establish that cells communicate through secreted signal molecules that act on specific receptors and regulate growth, survival, and differentiation. This discovery opened new fields of biology and medicine and influenced research on development, cancer, and tissue repair.

\section{Background and Historical Context}

The regulation of cell growth and differentiation is one of the central questions of biology. During embryonic development, a single fertilized egg gives rise to cells of many different types---neurons, muscle cells, blood cells, epithelial cells, and many others. This process of proliferation and differentiation must be regulated, while abnormal growth can contribute to cancer and developmental disease. How does the embryo control the growth and differentiation of its cells?

By the mid-twentieth century, it was known that cells in the body produce and respond to various chemical signals, but the nature of these signals---and the mechanisms by which they regulate cell growth---were poorly understood. The study of nerve growth, in particular, had fascinated biologists for decades. It was known that neurons in the developing embryo extend long axons to reach their target tissues, and that this process of axon growth depends on the availability of target-derived signals. However, the identity of these signals and the mechanisms by which they promote nerve growth were unknown.

Rita Levi-Montalcini was born in Turin, Italy, in 1909, and trained in medicine at the University of Turin. She began studying the development of the nervous system in the late 1940s, at a time when the study of neuroembryology was in its infancy. Stanley Cohen was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1922, and trained in biochemistry at Oberlin College and Washington University in St. Louis, where he joined the laboratory of Viktor Hamburger---a developmental biologist who had been studying nerve growth and differentiation for decades.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

\subsection{Rita Levi-Montalcini and the Discovery of Nerve Growth Factor}

Levi-Montalcini's journey to the discovery of nerve growth factor (NGF) began in the late 1930s and 1940s, when she studied the development of the peripheral nervous system in chick embryos. Working in Turin under wartime conditions, she performed experiments in which she removed or added limb-bud tissue and observed the effects on developing nerves. The results suggested that target tissues release a diffusible influence that promotes the growth and survival of appropriate neurons.

These observations led Levi-Montalcini to hypothesize that the target tissue produces a diffusible substance that promotes the survival and growth of neurons. She called this substance ``nerve growth factor,'' and she spent the next decade searching for it. In 1946, Levi-Montalcini was invited to work in Viktor Hamburger's laboratory at Washington University in St. Louis. She and Stanley Cohen began their close collaboration there in the early 1950s.

Cohen, a biochemist with expertise in protein purification, was tasked with isolating and characterizing the nerve growth factor that Levi-Montalcini had postulated. Using a bioassay that measured the effect of tissue extracts on nerve fiber growth in chick embryos, Cohen and Levi-Montalcini showed that a substance present in mouse salivary glands (and in snake venom) was a potent stimulator of nerve fiber growth. Cohen purified this substance and showed that it was a protein---the nerve growth factor (NGF).

The discovery of NGF was a landmark in biology. It was the first of many growth-regulating factors to be identified and demonstrated that secreted proteins can regulate neuronal growth and survival. Levi-Montalcini's bioassay---in which nerve-fiber growth in chick embryos was used to detect NGF activity---helped make its purification and characterization possible.

Levi-Montalcini and Cohen published their seminal paper on NGF in 1960, and the discovery was immediately recognized as a major advance. NGF was shown to promote the survival, growth, and differentiation of sympathetic and sensory neurons, and it was found to play a critical role in the development of the peripheral nervous system. The discovery of NGF also opened up a new field of research: the study of neurotrophic factors---the family of proteins that support the survival and growth of neurons.

\subsection{Stanley Cohen and the Discovery of Epidermal Growth Factor}

Cohen's second major discovery was the identification of epidermal growth factor (EGF), a protein that stimulates the growth and differentiation of epithelial cells. The discovery of EGF was serendipitous: while studying the effects of mouse salivary gland extracts on nerve growth, Cohen noticed that the extracts also caused premature opening of the eyelids and eruption of the incisor teeth in newborn mice. This observation led him to suspect that the salivary gland extracts contained a second growth factor---distinct from NGF---that stimulated the growth of epithelial tissues.

Cohen purified this second factor and named it epidermal growth factor (EGF). He showed that EGF was a small protein of 53 amino acids that stimulated epithelial-cell proliferation and other developmental responses. Cohen also showed that EGF acts through a specific cell-surface receptor, the EGF receptor (EGFR), whose tyrosine-specific kinase activity helped establish a general mechanism of receptor-mediated signal transduction.

The discovery of EGF and the EGFR pathway had important implications for cancer biology. EGFR overexpression, activating mutations, and abnormal downstream signaling occur in subsets of cancers. Subsequent research led to targeted therapies, including gefitinib and erlotinib for selected patients with EGFR-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer, as well as other EGFR-directed treatments.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The discoveries of Cohen and Levi-Montalcini have had a major impact on modern biology and medicine. Growth factors and the signaling pathways through which they act are central to understanding development, cancer, wound healing, and tissue repair.

In cancer biology, growth-factor signaling is one of several pathways that can become dysregulated. Cancers may overproduce ligands, overexpress receptors, or acquire activating changes in receptors and downstream signaling proteins. This understanding has supported targeted therapies, including trastuzumab for HER2-positive cancers and EGFR-directed treatments. Imatinib is also a targeted tyrosine-kinase inhibitor, but it primarily inhibits the BCR--ABL fusion kinase in chronic myeloid leukemia rather than a growth-factor receptor.

In wound healing and tissue repair, growth factors participate in inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases. Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-$\beta$) are among the mediators involved. Some recombinant growth-factor products have been used clinically in selected wounds; becaplermin (Regranex), a recombinant form of PDGF, was approved for certain diabetic foot ulcers, although use depends on indication, benefit, and safety considerations.

In regenerative medicine, growth factors are investigated to guide stem-cell differentiation, enhance tissue repair, and develop therapies for degenerative disease. Applications involving nerve tissue, bone, cartilage, and blood vessels remain active research areas, and many proposed uses have not yet become routine clinical treatments.

The EGFR pathway has been particularly important for targeted cancer therapy. EGFR-directed antibodies such as cetuximab and small-molecule inhibitors such as gefitinib and erlotinib block EGFR signaling in selected settings. Benefits vary by tumor type and molecular profile; EGFR inhibitors can improve outcomes for selected patients with lung, colorectal, and head-and-neck cancers but are not effective for every tumor.

Beyond cancer and wound healing, altered growth-factor signaling has been studied in diseases including atherosclerosis, diabetes, neurodegeneration, and age-related macular degeneration. These associations have generated therapeutic hypotheses, but growth-factor manipulation is not a general method for extending human lifespan.

\section{Legacy}

Rita Levi-Montalcini continued her work on growth factors and neurobiology at the National Research Council in Rome and at the Institute of Neurobiology. She became an international advocate for women in science and for education in developing countries. She died in 2012 at the age of 103, but her work continues to inspire scientists around the world.

Stanley Cohen continued his work at Vanderbilt University, where he made further contributions to the study of growth-factor signaling and cell proliferation. He died in 2020, but the discoveries of NGF and EGF remain major milestones in the history of biology.

The work of Cohen and Levi-Montalcini demonstrated that cell growth and differentiation can be regulated by specific molecules---growth factors---acting through cellular receptors. This principle became a foundation of modern cell biology, developmental biology, and cancer research, and it contributed to later therapies for cancer and selected tissue-repair conditions. Their discovery is also a testament to the value of curiosity-driven research and of following unexpected observations.


\section{Modern Developments}

Cohen and Levi-Montalcini's growth-factor work helped enable modern targeted cancer therapy. Growth-factor signaling informs drugs targeting EGFR (cetuximab), HER2 (trastuzumab), and VEGF (bevacizumab). Antibodies against NGF, including tanezumab, have been investigated for chronic pain but have faced efficacy and safety barriers and are not routine approved treatments. Growth-factor research also informed tyrosine-kinase inhibitors such as erlotinib; imatinib targets BCR--ABL rather than a growth-factor receptor.
