\section{Background and Historical Context}

Selman Abraham Waksman was born on July 22, 1888, in Priluki, Ukraine, then part of the Russian Empire. His family emigrated to the United States in 1910. He earned a bachelor's degree in agriculture from Rutgers University in 1911 and a Ph.D. in soil chemistry from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1915. He then returned to Rutgers, where he built a career studying soil microorganisms.

Waksman's research focused on the bacteria and fungi that inhabit soil and participate in decomposition and nutrient cycling. He argued that these organisms were also a promising source of antimicrobial compounds. This insight led him to develop a systematic program for isolating soil microbes and testing whether they inhibited disease-causing bacteria.

The medical context of the 1940s made this strategy especially important. Penicillin had shown the therapeutic power of antibiotics, but tuberculosis and many other infections still lacked effective treatment. Waksman's laboratory therefore searched for agents with activity against organisms, including \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis}, that were not adequately treated by penicillin.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

Selman Waksman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine ``for his discovery of streptomycin, the first antibiotic effective against tuberculosis.'' His contribution included organizing the soil-microbe screening program, directing the research, and helping develop streptomycin as a clinically useful antibiotic. The discovery itself was a collaborative achievement involving, among others, his graduate student Albert Schatz and laboratory colleague Elizabeth Bugie.

\subsection{The Search for Antibiotics from Soil Microorganisms}

Waksman's approach was systematic. His laboratory collected and cultured soil microorganisms, then tested their ability to inhibit pathogenic bacteria on agar plates. A clear zone around a microbial colony indicated that it produced an antibacterial substance worthy of further study. This screening strategy led to several antibiotics, including actinomycin, streptothricin, and grisein, and ultimately to streptomycin.

\subsection{The Discovery of Streptomycin}

In 1943, Albert Schatz isolated a strain of \textit{Streptomyces griseus} that produced a substance active against \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis}. Elizabeth Bugie contributed to the laboratory work, and Waksman directed the broader research program and helped name and develop the compound. The substance was called ``streptomycin.''

Streptomycin was the first antibiotic shown to be effective against tuberculosis. Early studies by William Feldman and Corwin Hinshaw at the Mayo Clinic in 1944 demonstrated activity in experimental and clinical tuberculosis. These results established streptomycin as a major advance, while later experience showed that resistance could emerge rapidly when it was used alone.

\subsection{The Chemistry and Mechanism of Action of Streptomycin}

Streptomycin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic. It binds to the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit and interferes with accurate translation, including by promoting misreading of messenger RNA. The resulting defective proteins can kill susceptible bacteria. Because this target differs from the cell-wall pathway affected by penicillin, streptomycin could act against some organisms that penicillin did not treat, including \textit{M.~tuberculosis}.

Resistance can arise through changes affecting streptomycin uptake, modification of the drug, or mutations in its ribosomal target. This limitation is one reason streptomycin is not used as a standalone treatment for tuberculosis.

\subsection{The Controversy Over Credit}

The discovery became the subject of a major dispute over scientific credit. Schatz had isolated the producing strain and performed key experiments leading to streptomycin, while Bugie was also a coauthor of the original report. Waksman's direction of the screening program and role in developing the discovery were substantial, but describing Schatz as merely a technician is inaccurate and minimizes his contribution.

In 1950, a legal settlement recognized Schatz as a co-discoverer for the relevant patent and royalty arrangements. The 1952 Nobel Prize was awarded solely to Waksman. The episode illustrates how credit can be distributed unevenly in collaborative research, especially between a senior investigator and a graduate student.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

Waksman's work changed the treatment of tuberculosis and helped establish systematic natural-product screening as a model for antibiotic discovery.

\subsection{Treatment of Tuberculosis}

Before streptomycin, tuberculosis was commonly managed with isolation and supportive care, and many patients died from the disease. Streptomycin provided the first effective antibacterial treatment, but it did not make tuberculosis reliably curable as a single-drug therapy. The rapid emergence of resistance helped drive the later use of combination regimens, including isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol.

Modern treatment depends on drug susceptibility, disease site, age, comorbidities, and local guidance. Streptomycin is now reserved for selected situations rather than serving as a routine first-line component of treatment for drug-susceptible tuberculosis. Its continuing limitations include resistance and toxicity.

\subsection{The Antibiotic Pipeline}

Waksman's demonstration that soil actinomycetes could yield clinically useful drugs influenced antibiotic discovery for decades. The genus \textit{Streptomyces} has produced many important natural products, including compounds that led to tetracyclines, macrolides, glycopeptides, and other antimicrobial classes. Although discovery methods now also include genome mining, synthetic biology, and metagenomics, environmental microorganisms remain an important source of drug candidates.

\subsection{Combination Chemotherapy}

Streptomycin's effectiveness, followed by the recognition that resistance developed readily during monotherapy, helped establish the need for combination chemotherapy in tuberculosis. Using drugs with different targets reduces the probability that a pathogen will survive with resistance to all of them. The principle has since become central to the treatment of many infectious diseases and is also used in cancer therapy.

\subsection{Adverse Effects and Limitations}

Streptomycin can cause ototoxicity, including permanent vestibular or hearing damage, and can also cause nephrotoxicity and neuromuscular adverse effects. These risks are especially important during prolonged treatment. As safer and more effective alternatives became available, streptomycin's role narrowed, although it remains useful in selected infections and resistance patterns.

\section{Legacy}

Selman Waksman's legacy combines a transformative medical discovery with a durable method for antibiotic research. Streptomycin opened effective antibacterial treatment for tuberculosis and demonstrated the value of systematically screening environmental microorganisms.

Waksman continued his research at Rutgers University after receiving the Nobel Prize and helped establish the university's Institute of Microbiology. He received numerous honors, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and died on August 16, 1973, at the age of 85.

The history of streptomycin also preserves the contributions of Schatz, Bugie, and the wider research team. As antimicrobial resistance grows, the episode remains a reminder that scientific credit, clinical development, and laboratory collaboration are distinct parts of a medical breakthrough.

\section{Modern Developments}

Current tuberculosis treatment uses multidrug regimens selected according to susceptibility and patient factors. For drug-susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis, the familiar six-month regimen remains an important option, while shorter alternatives are available for some patients under current guidelines. Streptomycin is not routinely required in these standard regimens.

For eligible patients with rifampicin-resistant or multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, the World Health Organization recommends shorter all-oral regimens in appropriate circumstances, including regimens built around bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid, with moxifloxacin added in BPaLM when indicated. Eligibility and drug selection depend on resistance testing, contraindications, and national guidance. Research continues into shorter, safer, and more accessible treatments.
