\section{Background and Historical Context}

Charles Robert Richet was born on August 26, 1850, in Paris, France, into a distinguished family of physicians and scientists. His father, Alfred Richet, was a prominent surgeon and professor at the University of Paris. The younger Richet studied medicine at the University of Paris, receiving his medical degree in 1876. He then pursued a career in experimental physiology, combining clinical medicine with laboratory research in a manner that was characteristic of the French medical tradition.

Richet's interest in the immune system was sparked by his work on the physiological effects of marine toxins. In 1901, during a cruise with Prince Albert I of Monaco, Richet and Paul Portier studied the venom of the Portuguese man-of-war (\emph{Physalia}) and later used extracts from the sea anemone \emph{Actinia}. Their experiments led to the recognition of anaphylaxis---a paradoxical and dangerous hypersensitivity reaction that could occur when the body was re-exposed to a substance to which it had been previously sensitized.\cite{richet-lecture,richet-history}

The concept of anaphylaxis---from the Greek \emph{ana}, against, and \emph{phylaxis}, protection---was, in the late nineteenth century, a deeply puzzling phenomenon. It was well established that the body could develop immunity to certain toxins and pathogens, and that this immunity was generally protective. But there were puzzling exceptions: some animals, when immunized with small doses of a toxin and then re-exposed to a larger dose, did not show enhanced resistance but instead developed severe, often fatal, reactions. This ``hypersensitivity'' or ``untoward reactivity'' was the opposite of what would be expected if the immune response were purely beneficial.

Several observations had hinted at the existence of hypersensitivity reactions before Richet's work. Clemens von Pirquet coined the term ``allergy'' in 1906 to describe altered reactivity, including reactions seen after serum therapy. Richet and Portier's controlled animal experiments gave the phenomenon a systematic experimental framework and supplied the term ``anaphylaxis.''\cite{richet-history}

\section{The Discovery}

Richet's discovery of anaphylaxis grew from experiments begun with Paul Portier in 1901 and continued by Richet after their collaboration ended. Experiments on dogs showed that animals surviving an initial dose of a toxin could develop a rapid, severe reaction when re-exposed weeks later.\cite{richet-lecture}

The key experiment, published in 1902 in collaboration with Paul Portier, used toxin extracts from the sea anemone \emph{Actinia} after their earlier work on \emph{Physalia}. Dogs that received a small, sublethal dose and were re-exposed several weeks later did not show the expected immunity. Instead, they developed a severe, often fatal reaction characterized by vomiting, diarrhea, collapse, and death. The reaction occurred rapidly after the second exposure.\cite{richet-lecture,richet-history}

Richet named this phenomenon ``anaphylaxis'' to distinguish it from ``prophylaxis'' (protection), the expected outcome of immunization. Subsequent experiments showed that anaphylaxis could be produced with many proteins and toxins, that sensitization was often antigen-specific, and that the state could be passively transferred through blood serum. The soluble factors involved were not understood in Richet's time; many forms of immediate allergy involve allergen-specific IgE and mediator release from mast cells and basophils, although anaphylaxis can also occur through non-IgE-mediated mechanisms.\cite{richet-lecture,richet-immunology}

Richet's experimental findings can be summarized as follows:

\textbf{Sensitization.} A small, sublethal dose of a foreign protein (the ``allergen'') administered to an animal produced a state of sensitization that was not associated with any visible symptoms. The animal appeared healthy and showed no signs of immune response.

\textbf{Elicitation.} When the sensitized animal was re-exposed to the same foreign protein, even in small doses, it developed a severe, potentially fatal reaction within minutes. The symptoms included bronchospasm (contraction of the airways), vasodilation and increased vascular permeability (leading to a drop in blood pressure and fluid leakage into tissues), gastrointestinal disturbance, and in severe cases, cardiovascular collapse and death.

\textbf{Specificity.} Richet's experiments showed that anaphylactic sensitivity was often specific to the substance used for sensitization. This observation anticipated, but did not by itself establish, the modern concept of antigen-specific antibodies.

\textbf{Transfer by serum.} Richet reported that anaphylactic sensitivity could be transferred from a sensitized animal to an unsensitized animal through serum. This demonstrated that a soluble blood factor was involved, but it did not identify that factor as IgE; the immunoglobulin responsible for many immediate allergic reactions was characterized decades later.\cite{richet-immunology}

Richet's findings helped establish that an immune response could cause disease as well as protect against it. The interpretation of anaphylaxis developed through subsequent work by researchers including Arthus, Rosenau, Anderson, and later immunologists who identified the cellular and molecular mediators of immediate hypersensitivity.\cite{richet-lecture,richet-immunology}

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

Richet's discovery of anaphylaxis helped lay the foundation for the modern study of allergic disease. Allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, atopic dermatitis, food allergy, drug allergy, and insect-sting allergy involve distinct but sometimes overlapping immune mechanisms; anaphylaxis is the acute, potentially life-threatening systemic reaction among them.

The understanding of anaphylaxis has led to treatments for allergic diseases. Antihistamines can relieve histamine-mediated symptoms but are not substitutes for epinephrine in anaphylaxis. Intramuscular epinephrine (adrenaline) is the first-line emergency treatment for anaphylaxis, and many people at risk carry it in auto-injectors such as EpiPens.

The understanding of the immunological basis of allergy has also led to the development of allergen-specific immunotherapy (desensitization therapy), in which patients are gradually exposed to increasing doses of the allergen that triggers their allergic response. This treatment, which is effective for insect sting allergies, allergic rhinitis, and some forms of asthma, is based on the principle of modifying the immune response to an allergen---a principle that can be traced directly to Richet's observations on anaphylaxis.

More broadly, Richet's work demonstrated that the immune response could be a double-edged sword---protective in some circumstances but harmful in others. This insight has had significant implications for the understanding of autoimmune diseases, in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissues, and for the development of immunosuppressive therapies that modulate the immune response in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions.

\section{Legacy}

Charles Richet received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1913 ``for his discovery of anaphylaxis.'' He was 63 years old and had been working on the phenomenon for over a decade. The award was a fitting recognition of one of the major discoveries in the history of immunology.

Richet continued his research after receiving the Nobel Prize, publishing extensively on physiology, immunology, and a wide range of other topics. He was a prolific and eclectic scientist whose interests extended beyond medicine to include psychology, philosophy, and literature. He died on December 4, 1935, at the age of 85.

Richet's discovery of anaphylaxis changed the understanding of the immune system from a purely defensive mechanism to a complex system with both beneficial and harmful potential. Modern allergy and emergency treatment draw on this history, although different allergic diseases involve different mechanisms and are not all forms of anaphylaxis. In recognizing the destructive potential of the immune response, Richet opened a new chapter in the understanding of human health and disease.


\section{Modern Developments}

Richet's discovery of anaphylaxis helped establish the modern allergy field. Intramuscular epinephrine remains first-line emergency treatment for anaphylaxis. Understanding IgE-mediated hypersensitivity has supported treatments such as anti-IgE therapy and allergen immunotherapy. In the United States, Palforzia is an FDA-approved oral immunotherapy for patients with confirmed peanut allergy; it is used to reduce reactions after accidental exposure, not as emergency treatment.\cite{richet-modern}
