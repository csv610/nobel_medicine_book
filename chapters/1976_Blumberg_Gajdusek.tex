The 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Baruch S. Blumberg and D. Carleton Gajdusek for their independent but complementary discoveries concerning new mechanisms for the origin and dissemination of infectious diseases. Blumberg's identification and characterization of the Australia antigen, later recognized as hepatitis B surface antigen, provided a marker for hepatitis B infection. Gajdusek demonstrated that kuru, a fatal neurodegenerative disease among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, was transmissible. Later work identified the causative agent of kuru as a prion; that was not a discovery made by Gajdusek alone.

\section{Background and Historical Context}

The study of infectious diseases is one of the oldest and most consequential branches of medicine. By the middle of the twentieth century, the germ theory of disease---the principle that many diseases are caused by microorganisms---was well established, and major advances in bacteriology, virology, and parasitology had led to the development of vaccines, antibiotics, and public health measures that dramatically reduced the burden of infectious disease in the industrialized world. Yet major questions remained unanswered. Some chronic liver diseases and degenerative neurological conditions could not be attributed to a known pathogen. The mechanisms by which some diseases were transmitted---and the agents responsible for them---remained obscure.

Baruch Stanley Blumberg was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1925, to a family of Eastern European Jewish immigrants. He trained in medicine at Columbia University and later pursued graduate studies in biochemistry and genetics at Oxford University. In the late 1950s, Blumberg began a systematic study of genetic variation in human populations, collecting blood samples from diverse populations around the world. His interest was in understanding how genetic polymorphisms might influence susceptibility to disease---a question that was at the forefront of human genetics at the time.

D. Carleton Gajdusek was born in Yonkers, New York, in 1923, and trained in medicine at Harvard Medical School. A voracious reader and an adventurous traveler, Gajdusek was drawn to the study of infectious diseases in tropical and remote regions of the world. In the 1950s and 1960s, he became fascinated by a devastating neurological disease called kuru, which was decimating the Fore people of the Eastern Highlands of Papua New Guinea. Kuru caused progressive cerebellar ataxia, tremors, and death, usually within months to about a year after symptom onset. It was one of the great medical mysteries of the twentieth century.

The historical context of both discoveries was shaped by the post-World War II expansion of global health research. Organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO), the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and the Rockefeller Foundation were supporting research into infectious diseases in developing countries, and there was a growing recognition that many diseases that had been neglected by Western medicine could yield important insights into fundamental biology.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

\subsection{Baruch Blumberg and the Australia Antigen}

Blumberg's work on hepatitis B began with his study of genetic polymorphisms in human blood proteins. In the late 1950s, Blumberg and colleagues, working at the Institutes of the National Institutes of Health and later at the Fox Chase Cancer Center, collected blood samples from populations around the world. They used techniques including agar-gel immunodiffusion and immunoelectrophoresis to identify previously unknown variations in serum proteins.

In 1965, Blumberg and Harvey Alter detected a previously unknown antigen in a serum sample from an Australian Aboriginal person when it was tested against blood from a multiply transfused patient. The antigen was designated ``Australia antigen'' (Au antigen). Blumberg initially suspected that it was a genetic variant of a serum protein, but later studies connected it with serum hepatitis.

Through studies conducted between 1966 and 1968, Blumberg and colleagues showed that the Australia antigen was associated with serum hepatitis and was a component of the hepatitis B virus envelope. It became known as hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and provided a serological marker for infection; the complete characterization of HBV involved later work by many researchers.

The identification of HBsAg had immediate public-health implications. Before reliable serological markers were available, screening blood donations for hepatitis B was not possible; HBsAg testing made blood-supply screening feasible and reduced transfusion-associated transmission. The discovery also supported development of hepatitis B vaccines by later researchers and manufacturers. Hepatitis B vaccination became the first vaccination recognized to prevent a human cancer indirectly, because prevention of chronic HBV infection reduces the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Blumberg's work also had broader implications for the understanding of the relationship between infectious agents and cancer. The demonstration that a virus could cause a chronic infection that, in turn, could lead to cancer, was a landmark in cancer research. It confirmed the hypothesis, first proposed by Peyton Rous and others, that viruses could play a role in human cancer, and it provided a model system for studying the molecular mechanisms by which chronic infection leads to malignant transformation.

\subsection{D. Carleton Gajdusek and the Discovery of Kuru as an Infectious Disease}

Gajdusek's discovery of the infectious nature of kuru is one of the most notable stories in the history of medicine. Kuru was first described by Western medicine in the 1950s, when Australian administrators and missionaries in the Eastern Highlands of Papua New Guinea reported a mysterious disease affecting the Fore people and neighboring tribes. The disease caused a progressive loss of coordination (ataxia), uncontrollable trembling (hence the name ``kuru,'' which in the Fore language means ``to shiver'' or ``to tremble with fear''), emotional lability, and ultimately death, usually within six to twelve months of symptom onset.

Gajdusek first visited Papua New Guinea in 1957, at the invitation of Australian patrol officers who were concerned about the devastating effects of kuru on the Fore population. He was immediately struck by the epidemiological pattern of the disease: kuru occurred predominantly among women and children of the Fore tribe, and it appeared to be transmitted within families. The disease was not contagious in the conventional sense---close physical contact with kuru patients did not appear to spread the disease---but it was clearly infectious in some manner.

Working with Australian colleague Vincent Zigas and with anthropologists and local communities, Gajdusek conducted epidemiological and pathological studies of kuru. The disease was associated with a Fore funerary practice involving consumption of the dead, with women and children particularly exposed to infected tissues. Gajdusek and other investigators concluded that this practice provided a route for transmission, while recognizing that the epidemiology also reflected social roles and exposure patterns.

To test whether kuru was transmissible, Gajdusek and colleagues inoculated chimpanzees with brain tissue from kuru patients. After an incubation period of roughly one and a half to three years, some chimpanzees developed a neurological disease resembling kuru. This demonstrated transmission from humans to nonhuman primates and provided powerful evidence for an infectious agent.

However, the nature of this infectious agent remained a significant mystery. The agent showed unusual resistance to some procedures that inactivate many conventional pathogens, although resistance depended on the treatment conditions and did not by itself prove that the agent lacked nucleic acid. Gajdusek initially described kuru as an ``unconventional virus'' or ``slow virus,'' reflecting the long incubation period; later work led to the prion hypothesis.

The prion hypothesis was developed later, particularly through the work of Stanley Prusiner and many other researchers. Kuru is now classified as a prion disease, along with other transmissible spongiform encephalopathies such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and scrapie. Gajdusek's transmission studies provided an essential foundation, but they did not by themselves establish that the infectious agent was a protein without nucleic acid.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

The discoveries of Blumberg and Gajdusek have had far-reaching consequences for modern medicine. Blumberg's identification of the Australia antigen, later recognized as HBsAg, enabled blood-screening tests that reduce transfusion-associated hepatitis B. Hepatitis B vaccination, developed later and now recommended for infants in many national programs, reduces chronic HBV infection and consequently lowers the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. The size of the benefit depends on vaccination coverage, timing of the birth dose, and access to testing and treatment.

The hepatitis B vaccine is also notable as the first vaccine recognized to prevent cancer indirectly by preventing infection with an oncogenic virus. This principle was later applied to human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, which reduces infections that can cause cervical and other cancers.

Gajdusek's work on kuru helped open the field of prion-disease research. Kuru, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, Gerstmann--Str\"aussler--Scheinker syndrome, and other transmissible spongiform encephalopathies are now understood within prion biology, although inherited, sporadic, and acquired forms have different origins. Prion diseases are fatal and currently lack curative treatment. The emergence of bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, or ``mad cow disease'') and variant Creutzfeldt--Jakob disease further demonstrated the public-health importance of this field.

The prion hypothesis has influenced research on other neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases, in which misfolded proteins and cell-to-cell spread are being studied. These disorders are not thereby established to be infectious prion diseases; the analogy concerns possible molecular propagation mechanisms.

Moreover, Gajdusek's work on the epidemiology of kuru in Papua New Guinea demonstrated the importance of cultural and behavioral factors in disease transmission---a lesson that remains relevant for emerging and re-emerging infections. Recognition that funerary practices, dietary habits, and other cultural practices can influence disease spread has informed public-health interventions, including safer burial practices during Ebola outbreaks and surveillance for variant Creutzfeldt--Jakob disease.

\section{Legacy}

Baruch Blumberg's legacy extends well beyond his Nobel Prize-winning work on hepatitis B. After the discovery of HBsAg, he continued work on hepatitis, cancer prevention, global health, and the relationship between infectious agents and chronic disease. He later contributed to NASA's astrobiology program and served as the founding director of the NASA Astrobiology Institute. He did not found the National Human Genome Research Institute or the Institute for Systems Biology. Blumberg died in 2011, but his influence on hepatitis research and global health continues to be felt.

D. Carleton Gajdusek's scientific legacy rests on his epidemiological, pathological, and transmission studies of kuru and related diseases. Those studies demonstrated that a fatal neurodegenerative disease could be transmissible despite its long incubation period and lack of conventional inflammatory signs; they did not establish the later prion mechanism. His personal life was also marked by a criminal conviction for child sexual abuse, a serious matter distinct from the assessment of his scientific work. He died in 2008.

The work of both laureates illustrates how studying unusual or neglected diseases and populations can reveal general biological principles. Blumberg's study of human blood variation led to the Australia antigen and advances in hepatitis B research. Gajdusek's investigation of kuru led to the demonstration of transmissibility and helped create the foundation on which later prion research was built.


\section{Modern Developments}

Blumberg and Gajdusek's work helped establish modern hepatitis B control and prion research. HBsAg testing supports blood safety and diagnosis, while hepatitis B vaccination reduces chronic infection and the resulting risk of liver cancer. Prion biology informs research on Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases, which may involve prion-like protein propagation but are not classified as infectious prion diseases. Antiviral drugs for hepatitis B, including entecavir and tenofovir, suppress viral replication but usually do not eradicate the virus, motivating research toward functional cures.
