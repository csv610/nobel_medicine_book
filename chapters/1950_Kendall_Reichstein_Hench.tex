\section{Official Citation}

for their discoveries relating to the hormones of the adrenal cortex, their structure and biological effects

\section{Background and Historical Context}

Edward Calvin Kendall was born in South Norwalk, Connecticut, in 1886. He studied chemistry at Columbia University and joined the Mayo Foundation, where access to adrenal glands from slaughterhouses made large-scale extraction and analysis possible.

Tadeus Reichstein was born in W\l{}oc\l{}awek in 1897, then part of the Russian Empire, and grew up in Switzerland. He studied chemistry at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zurich and later worked at the University of Basel. His research on natural products led him to the chemistry of adrenal-cortex steroids.

Philip Showalter Hench was born in Pittsburgh in 1896. He trained in medicine and became a rheumatologist at the Mayo Clinic. He was interested in temporary remissions of rheumatoid arthritis associated with pregnancy, jaundice, and other physiological states, and suspected that an adrenal-related substance might be involved.

In the first half of the twentieth century, the adrenal cortex was known to be essential for life, but its hormones had not been fully isolated or related to their biological effects. The challenge required chemical purification, structural analysis, physiological testing, and clinical observation.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

The three laureates made complementary contributions. Kendall isolated and characterized several adrenal steroids and worked with Hench on their clinical use. Reichstein independently carried out major chemical studies, including isolation, structural analysis, and synthesis. Hench demonstrated the striking but temporary anti-inflammatory effect of cortisone in rheumatoid arthritis.

\subsection{Kendall's Adrenal Steroids}

Kendall and his colleagues separated adrenal extracts into compounds designated by letters. Compound E was later named cortisone; compound F was hydrocortisone, also called cortisol. The compounds were not interchangeable: cortisone and cortisol have different oxidation states and biological properties, although both belong to the glucocorticoid family.

The isolation of these substances made it possible to study their chemical structures and physiological actions. It also provided enough material for the later development of therapeutic preparations. The work did not mean that Kendall discovered a single ``stress hormone'' or that all adrenal steroids have the same function.

\subsection{Reichstein's Chemical Work}

Reichstein and his collaborators developed methods for extracting and characterizing adrenal-cortex steroids and their metabolites. Their work helped establish relationships among steroid structure, biological activity, and chemical transformation. Reichstein also developed routes for partial synthesis, making scarce natural compounds more accessible for research and treatment.

The chemistry of the adrenal cortex was a collaborative field. Several laboratories isolated related compounds, and the eventual therapeutic supply depended on later industrial synthesis and fermentation-derived starting materials. Reichstein's Nobel-recognized contribution was the systematic chemical foundation, not sole ownership of every isolation or synthetic step.

\subsection{Hench and Cortisone in Rheumatoid Arthritis}

Hench had observed that rheumatoid arthritis could temporarily improve under conditions such as pregnancy or jaundice. In 1948, after sufficient compound E became available, he and Mayo colleagues Charles Slocumb and Howard Polley administered it to a severely affected 29-year-old patient. The patient's improvement over the following days was dramatic. Subsequent treatment of additional patients and the 1949 report confirmed a powerful anti-inflammatory effect, but not a cure.

Cortisone soon became an important treatment for rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory diseases. Its benefits could be striking but were often temporary, and prolonged or high-dose treatment produced substantial adverse effects. The later development of hydrocortisone, prednisone, prednisolone, dexamethasone, and other glucocorticoids improved potency, duration, and route of administration, but did not eliminate steroid toxicity.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

Glucocorticoids act through intracellular glucocorticoid receptors that regulate transcription and alter inflammatory and immune responses. This mechanism was clarified after the Nobel-winning work. Corticosteroids are used in asthma, allergic disease, autoimmune disorders, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatic disease, transplant medicine, and many other settings, with the choice and dose tailored to the condition.

The clinical value of these drugs must be balanced against risks such as infection, osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, hypertension, cataracts, skin changes, adrenal suppression, and mood effects. Abrupt withdrawal after prolonged systemic treatment can be dangerous because the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis may be suppressed. Topical, inhaled, intra-articular, and short systemic courses can reduce exposure in suitable patients but are not free of risk.

The study of adrenal hormones also supports diagnosis and treatment of adrenal insufficiency, including Addison disease. Replacement usually requires physiologic glucocorticoid dosing and, in primary adrenal failure, may also require mineralocorticoid replacement. These modern regimens are based on later endocrinology as well as on the foundational chemistry and physiology of the laureates.

Cortisone and related drugs are anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive treatments, not cures for the underlying causes of most autoimmune diseases. Their dramatic early effects led to overenthusiastic use, and subsequent clinical experience established the importance of controlled trials, dose minimization, monitoring, and steroid-sparing therapy.

\section{Legacy}

Kendall, Reichstein, and Hench illustrate how a medical advance can require several kinds of expertise. Chemical isolation alone did not establish therapeutic value, and a dramatic clinical response could not explain the hormone's structure or mechanism. Their work connected adrenal physiology, steroid chemistry, rheumatology, and pharmacology.

Kendall later worked on thyroid and other endocrine biochemistry and died in 1972. Reichstein continued research in natural-product chemistry at Basel and died in 1996. Hench continued at the Mayo Clinic and died in 1965. Their shared legacy includes both the benefits of glucocorticoid therapy and the lesson that powerful anti-inflammatory effects must be evaluated alongside long-term harm.

\section{Modern Developments}

Modern endocrinology uses cortisol measurements, stimulation tests, imaging, and molecular studies to diagnose adrenal disorders. Glucocorticoid research seeks tissue-selective actions and safer delivery, but selective glucocorticoid-receptor modulators remain an area of investigation rather than a universal replacement for established steroids. Treatments for Cushing syndrome include surgery and selected medicines such as osilodrostat or pasireotide, depending on the cause; these later therapies should not be attributed directly to the 1950 laureates.
