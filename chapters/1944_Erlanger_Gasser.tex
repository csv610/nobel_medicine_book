\section{Background and Historical Context}

Joseph Erlanger was born in San Francisco in 1874. He studied at the University of Wisconsin and received his medical degree from Johns Hopkins in 1899. After early work at Johns Hopkins, he joined Washington University in St. Louis, where he became professor and later head of physiology.

Herbert Spencer Gasser was born in Platteville, Wisconsin, in 1888. He studied at the University of Wisconsin and completed medical training at Johns Hopkins. He joined Erlanger's laboratory at Washington University, beginning a collaboration that combined physiology, instrumentation, and quantitative analysis of nerve signals. Gasser later held posts at Cornell University Medical College and the Rockefeller Institute.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, an electrical response recorded from a whole nerve was understood to be a compound signal, but its components were difficult to separate. Researchers needed better temporal resolution to determine whether different fibers conducted impulses at different speeds and whether those differences had functional significance.

\section{The Discovery/Contribution}

Erlanger and Gasser received the 1944 Nobel Prize for showing that nerve fibers have highly differentiated electrical and physiological properties. Their work concerned the analysis of compound action potentials in mixed nerves. It did not mean that they directly recorded and identified every individual sensory fiber or that each fiber carries only one kind of information.

\subsection{The Cathode-Ray Oscillograph}

The investigators adapted a cathode-ray oscillograph, together with amplification and recording methods, to display the rapid voltage changes produced by nerve stimulation. This equipment made it possible to distinguish successive components of a compound action potential that had previously appeared as a single, poorly resolved signal.

In experiments on isolated nerves from animals, they found that the waveform changed as the recording distance and stimulus conditions changed. The separate elevations reflected groups of fibers conducting at different velocities. The method was a major advance in neurophysiology and became an early example of using electronic instrumentation to measure biological signals with millisecond-scale timing.

\subsection{Fiber Diameter, Myelination, and Conduction Velocity}

Erlanger and Gasser showed that larger fibers generally conduct impulses faster than smaller fibers. Myelinated fibers also conduct more rapidly than many unmyelinated fibers. The relationship is not one universal constant for every fiber type, but it provided a powerful way to relate anatomy to electrophysiology.

Their recordings revealed that the response of a mixed nerve contains groups with different speeds. The fastest components were associated mainly with large, myelinated fibers; slower components came from smaller myelinated or unmyelinated fibers. This finding helped explain how the nervous system can transmit signals with different timing and functional roles through the same nerve trunk.

\subsection{A, B, and C Fiber Groups}

The A, B, and C terminology arose from this analysis and was refined by later work. In broad modern usage:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fibers} are myelinated and relatively fast. Subgroups such as A$\alpha$, A$\beta$, A$\gamma$, and A$\delta$ differ in size, speed, and typical function.
\item \textbf{B fibers} are small myelinated fibers, especially associated with preganglionic autonomic axons.
\item \textbf{C fibers} are unmyelinated and slow. They include fibers carrying some diffuse pain and temperature signals and some postganglionic autonomic traffic.
\end{itemize}

The classification describes populations of fibers and their conduction properties; it is not a complete map from fiber class to a single sensation. Later physiological and anatomical studies assigned more specific functions to many of the subgroups.

\subsection{Limits of the Original Interpretation}

The experiments established differences in conduction and electrical response, not the complete cellular mechanism of saltatory conduction. The modern account of saltatory conduction through nodes of Ranvier developed from later anatomical, biophysical, and intracellular studies. Likewise, the detailed relation between fiber classes and sensations such as touch, pain, and temperature was refined by subsequent researchers.

\section{Impact on Modern Medicine}

Nerve-conduction studies and electromyography use the same general principle: the timing and shape of evoked electrical responses provide information about peripheral nerves. Clinicians can use conduction velocity, latency, amplitude, and waveform to help distinguish demyelinating from axonal neuropathies and to assess conditions such as carpal tunnel syndrome, polyneuropathy, and radiculopathy. These tests measure populations of fibers rather than directly reading a patient's sensations.

The classification of fibers also supports the physiology of pain and anesthesia. A$\delta$ and C fibers commonly contribute to different components of nociception, while larger fibers carry other sensory and motor signals. Local anesthetics produce differential block that depends on fiber diameter, myelination, activity, and drug exposure; it is too simple to say that they always block only small unmyelinated fibers first. This physiology helps explain the effects of regional and neuraxial anesthesia.

The work also influenced research on demyelinating disease and peripheral nerve injury. Loss or slowing of conduction can result from damage to myelin, the axon, or the neuromuscular connection, and modern testing uses several measurements to distinguish these mechanisms. The connection to cardiac electrophysiology is indirect: cardiac muscle has its own specialized conduction system and should not be presented as a direct application of the Erlanger--Gasser nerve-fiber classification.

\section{Legacy}

Erlanger and Gasser's central achievement was methodological as well as physiological. By resolving the compound action potential, they made it possible to connect the size and structure of nerve fibers with the speed of electrical signaling. Their work helped establish quantitative neurophysiology as a field and supplied terminology still used in peripheral nerve research and clinical testing.

Their results were also collaborative and cumulative. Earlier investigators had studied nerve conduction and the carotid or spinal pathways, and later researchers clarified myelin, nodes of Ranvier, ionic currents, and sensory coding. The Nobel Prize recognized Erlanger and Gasser's decisive analysis within that broader history.

\section{Modern Developments}

Modern nerve-conduction studies, high-density electrophysiology, microneurography, and peripheral nerve interfaces extend the questions Erlanger and Gasser opened. Researchers now study selective stimulation, regeneration, neuropathic pain, and interfaces that decode or modulate peripheral nerve activity. These technologies build on the distinction between fiber populations while recognizing that nerve signals are distributed, dynamic, and context-dependent.
