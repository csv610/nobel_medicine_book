\section{Background and Historical Context}

Karl Landsteiner was born on June 14, 1868, in Vienna. He studied medicine at the University of Vienna and then pursued chemistry and immunology in laboratories in Zurich, Würzburg, and Munich. He returned to Vienna, worked in pathological anatomy and hygiene, and investigated the specificity of immune reactions. In 1908 he became prosector at the Wilhelminenspital, and in 1911 he became professor of pathological anatomy at the University of Vienna.

Landsteiner moved to The Hague in 1919 and joined the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York in 1922. His work ranged well beyond blood groups, including haptens, immunochemistry, syphilis serology, and poliomyelitis. His Nobel Prize recognized the blood-group discovery, not all of his later immunological work.

Before blood groups were understood, transfusion could produce severe reactions including shock, jaundice, and hemoglobinuria. Landsteiner's key insight was that human blood is not immunologically identical between individuals: serum from one person can agglutinate the red cells of another. This was an immunological incompatibility, not merely an experimental artifact.

\section{Discovery of the ABO System}

In 1901 Landsteiner mixed red cells and serum from different people and identified a reproducible pattern of agglutination. He initially described three groups, which he called A, B, and C. Subsequent work by Landsteiner and other investigators established the four groups now called A, B, AB, and O. The AB group was identified by Alfredo von Decastello and Adriano Sturli in 1902; group C was later renamed O.

The ABO system is based on inherited carbohydrate antigens on red-cell membranes and naturally occurring antibodies in plasma. Group A red cells carry A antigen and the plasma usually contains anti-B; group B red cells carry B antigen and the plasma usually contains anti-A; group AB red cells carry both antigens and usually lack anti-A and anti-B; group O red cells carry neither A nor B antigen and the plasma usually contains both antibodies. These simplified descriptions apply to routine ABO typing, while real transfusion practice also considers other antibodies and blood-group systems.

Landsteiner's experiments explained why transfusing incompatible red cells can cause agglutination and hemolysis. In modern practice, donor red cells must be compatible with antibodies in the recipient's plasma, and compatibility testing is required before transfusion except in carefully managed emergencies. ABO incompatibility can produce a severe or fatal hemolytic transfusion reaction.

\section{The Rh System and Other Work}

Landsteiner did not discover the ABO system alone in its final four-group form, and he did not discover the Rh system by himself. In 1940, working with Alexander S. Wiener, he described the Rh factor in experiments involving rhesus monkeys; later work showed that the human Rh system is more complex than the original factor. RhD compatibility is especially important in transfusion and pregnancy because anti-D antibodies can cause hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn.

Landsteiner also developed the concept of haptens: small chemical groups that can become immunologically active when attached to larger carrier molecules. This work helped establish the chemical specificity of antigen-antibody reactions and influenced later immunochemistry. It should not, however, be presented as though he directly invented every modern immunoassay, monoclonal antibody, or vaccine technology.

\section{The Nobel Prize and Immediate Impact}

Landsteiner received the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine ``for his discovery of human blood groups.'' The practical consequence was the ability to select compatible donors and recipients instead of treating transfusion as an unpredictable last resort. The Nobel recognition concerned the human blood groups and their significance, while the later development of blood banking, crossmatching, component therapy, and infection screening required contributions from many investigators.

\section{Impact on Modern Transfusion Medicine}

ABO and Rh typing remain essential parts of transfusion practice. Blood banks determine the patient's ABO and RhD type, screen for clinically significant antibodies, and perform compatibility testing before issuing blood. Red-cell, plasma, and platelet components have different compatibility requirements; there is no single blood type that is universally suitable for every component or every patient.

The expression ``universal donor'' is therefore an oversimplification. O-negative red cells may be used in some emergencies when the patient's type is not yet known, but they are not risk-free and do not remove the need for definitive typing and crossmatching. Other blood-group antigens, antibodies, patient history, and the component being transfused all matter.

Landsteiner's discovery also supported the development of transfusion services and helped establish blood groups as inherited traits. Blood-group analysis was once useful in excluding some possible biological relationships and in forensic screening, but DNA profiling is now far more discriminating for identification and paternity testing.

\section{Legacy}

Landsteiner's enduring achievement was to connect a visible laboratory reaction---red-cell agglutination---with a clinically important incompatibility between people. His work made transfusion safer and helped create immunohematology as a discipline. The history also illustrates the collaborative nature of biomedical discovery: Landsteiner's first observations, the identification of AB and O nomenclature, the Rh work with Wiener, and later blood-bank technology were distinct stages.

Landsteiner died in New York on June 26, 1943. His name remains associated with the ABO system, Rh-related research, and the broader principle that antigen-antibody specificity can be measured and used in clinical practice.

\section{Modern Developments}

Modern transfusion safety combines ABO and RhD typing with antibody screening, crossmatching, infectious-disease testing, careful patient identification, and surveillance for adverse reactions. Molecular blood-group genotyping can help resolve complex or recently transfused cases and can improve matching for patients who need repeated transfusions.

Blood groups are also studied in relation to disease susceptibility, but associations do not establish that a blood type protects against or causes a disease. Claims about ABO type and infections such as COVID-19 should therefore not be presented as settled clinical conclusions. The central modern lesson remains the one Landsteiner established: compatibility between donor red-cell antigens and recipient antibodies is fundamental to safe transfusion.
