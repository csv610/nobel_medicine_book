# Mentor Guide: Helping Students Find Their Question

The mentor's job is to open doors, not to assign one narrow path. Begin by showing students that “medicine” includes many connected subfields: the body, cells, genes, behavior, tools, treatments, populations, and ethics. Then invite each student to choose a laureate chapter whose opening question catches their imagination.

## A welcoming first conversation

Ask students:

1. What part of the human body or human experience makes you curious?
2. Do you prefer building a model, analyzing data, learning history, or designing a solution?
3. What problem would you want to help solve if you had a laboratory, clinic, or public-health team?

Tell students that they do not need to understand every word before beginning. They need one question they genuinely want to answer.

## The eight subfields

- [Infection, Immunity, and Vaccines](subfields/infection_immunity.md): how disease spreads and how bodies defend themselves.
- [Genetics, Genomics, and Development](subfields/genetics_development.md): how biological information is stored, regulated, inherited, and used to build an organism.
- [Neuroscience, Senses, and Behavior](subfields/neuroscience_behavior.md): how nerve cells, brains, senses, memory, and behavior work.
- [Physiology, Metabolism, and Endocrinology](subfields/physiology_metabolism.md): how organs and chemical pathways keep a body alive and balanced.
- [Cell Biology and Molecular Systems](subfields/cell_molecular.md): how cells organize materials, signals, proteins, and internal machinery.
- [Therapeutics, Surgery, and Public Health](subfields/therapeutics_public_health.md): how discoveries become prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and policy.
- [Reproduction, Aging, and Human Origins](subfields/reproduction_origins.md): how bodies and populations change across generations and time.
- [Medical Technology and Research Methods](subfields/technology_methods.md): how instruments, measurements, and experimental designs make new knowledge possible.

A laureate may fit more than one field. The course gives each chapter one primary home so students can browse easily; mentors should encourage connections across fields.

## How to introduce a chapter

1. Read the bold “Why this matters” hook aloud.
2. Ask students what they predict the laureate discovered.
3. Read the Nobel-recognized contribution, then compare it with the prediction.
4. Point out the chapter's mentor question before students read the source chapter.
5. Let students choose whether to complete the project alone, with a partner, or as a small team.

## Keep curiosity alive

Praise good questions, not just correct answers. When a student says “I don't understand,” ask which word, step, or assumption is blocking them. Connect unfamiliar science to something tangible: a signal, a map, a lock, a filter, a traffic network, a family trait, or a decision about risk.

Avoid presenting Nobel winners as solitary geniuses. Highlight assistants, collaborators, patients, communities, instruments, criticism, and later researchers. This helps students see science as a human practice they can join.

## A mentor's recurring questions

- What was observed before the explanation was accepted?
- What comparison makes the claim convincing?
- What could the method not measure?
- Who benefited, and who might bear risk?
- What would change your mind?
- What safe model could we build to explore the idea?
